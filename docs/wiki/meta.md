## Theme

### Agency

The character agency in Shelder Evolution is considered 'bleak', meaning that the choices and actions of the characters have some impact in the world, but it is easily forgotten and often only affects a limited sphere around that character.

An example would be that a character's actions might affect their friends and family, and other characters in the vicinity, in the community, in the ecoystem. Entities far away or much later in time might never know or only know of very little traces of what happened before.

This is in contrast with 'epic' agency, where main characters have long lasting impact in the world and everyone knows about them.

ShelderEvo doesn't much care for that 'main character energy', and treats everyone the same. There might be individuals with more influence over others, but influence is built over time and with much effort, often with cunning abilities or advantageous circumstances, or both.

### Tone

The tone in Shelder Evolution is considered 'grey', meaning that there is no clear 'good' or 'evil'; characters make their own choices, based on their own agency and beliefs. These characters might find it very hard to 'win', where even the concept of 'winning' is difficult to define, as it might only apply to their own situation, their own goal, and not everybody else's. Characters might also 'lose' often, where 'losing' is usually associated with loss, either of valuable possessions, relationships, or life itself, and they might lose out of purely random accidents, or circumstances out of their control.

This is contrast with 'bright' tone, where things are generally pleasent, beautiful, and where the uglyness, the gore, pain and suffering are rare or absent, and with 'dark' tone, where things are generally unpleasent, ugly, and where the beauty and comfort are rare or absent.
Grey lives in the middle, has elements of both extremes in roughly the same amount, and characters must navigate the complexity and the risk by exercising constant caution. The naive often pays for their naivety, but this doesn't mean that the sage is safe.

ShelderEvo applies 'grey' tone just by being tryng to adhere to the 'hard sci-fi' genre (see ['genre'](#genre)), where the uncaring scientific nature of the universe is blind to what happens to the characters. There is no 'plot-armor'; perma-death is probably the main mechanic to provide this tone, as well as the anymosity of raising what essentially amount to 'procedurally generated' characters: even when a creature is the offspring of someone of relevance, they are still treated by the game as any other creature. Even 'N.P.C.s' are treated the same as creatures adopted by players, thus making them 'NPCs' just by the fact that they aren't chaperoned by a player.

### Genre

The genre of Shelder Evolution can be broadly considered to be 'sci-fi' (science-fiction), with an attempt at 'hard sci-fi', which means there is an effort to provide plausible explanations for the phenomena in the world, and for the technology used. Some concessions, like the tech called 'HOC' (see [HOC](./docs/spec/hoc.md)) will likely push the fringe of what is considered 'hard sci-fi', but most other aspects of the story (and the game) can be considered as realistic as possible, if anything, a plausible approximation.

## Game

### Game genre

Shelder Evolution is designed to be a 'MMO' (Massive Multiplayer Online), but it can fall into both a 'RPG' (Role-Playing Game) and 'TCG' (Trading Card Game) term, because it is indeed both a game involving the progression of a character that the player invests into, and a card game where the player's card pool is variable and dependant on acquiring cards by various means.

### Meta-game

One aspect of Shelder Evolution that attempts to unify the lore with gameplay concepts like 'metagame' (see ['The Many Types of Metagaming' by SupergeekMike](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=drjCzU24gks)) and other forms of 'cheating' by leveraging some meta-narattive devices that essentially turn those 'cheats' into valid tactics within the game.

**Metagame as a killer of immersion?**

ShelderEvo is primarly a RPG ('Role-playing game', see [game genre](#game-genre)), so, immersion is an important part of playing that role. Metagame tends to break this immersion because it violates the boundaries of reality within the universe of the game. Everytime a player uses their own knowledge of the game in place of their character knowledge, they are committing metagame, simply because the character shouldn't have access to that knowledge in their own universe — the player is providing the character with that knowledge in what is essentially a 4th wall break. If you are immersed in the story and seeing it through the eyes of that character, the knowledge is essentially spawning into their head like magic.

This can also have implications of fairness, and thus, can also be considered 'cheating', and we'll come to this later, but, focusing on the immersion aspect, metagame is often frowned upon in RPG games, and for good reason, but ShelderEvo attempts to mitigate the unpleasent feelings caused by metagame by using the lore to 'justify' the most commont acts of metagaming.

**How can metagame ever be justified?**

ShelderEvo isn't really justifying metagame per-se, but its implication within the lore: the player is also a player character within the game — it is literally called 'PLAYER' in the lore — and it is through the connection provided by the 'HOC' (see [HOC](./docs/spec/hoc.md)) devices that a player is capable of communicating with a creature. The gameplay is about this player-to-creature connection. So, because ShelderEvo as a game is also a game within the lore of the game, and players are also players within the lore of the game, it is entirely possible for these player characters to have knowledge outside of the creature perspective, and that player will inevitably pass on that knowledge onto the creature.

What this means is that, while it can still be considered 'unfair', it is technically not 'cheating' for a creature to know things beyond their reach. How ShelderEvo mitigates the unfairness is by using a system of EXP (experience, see [exp](./docs/spec/exp.md)) to make knowledge tangible and part of the mechanics, instead of abstract information. The gamification of knowledge allows for an extra layer of strategy which can be weaponized in gameplay, so that players — and their creatures — can both exploit with and, more importantly, defend against.

**Is this cheating?**

Cheating is when you deliberately exploit gaps in the rules (or you break them outright) to get an advantage. Metagame falls under 'cheating' because of the knowledge disconnect between the player's reality and that of their played role (in our case, the adopted creature). But, as we saw above, it is valid for a creature to inherit knowledge of the player, either directly, in the form of an experience card, or indirectly, because the player is guiding the creature into situations that the player knows how to manipulate. Both these approaches are valid, and we will attempt to make a case that they can also be considered 'fair', because the lore allows for this asymmetric exchange of information, but it also requires you to explicitly act upon it, by guiding the creature through the tactic that you are trying to employ. This final layer of creature agency will often act as an organic filter for 'unfair metagaming', because the creature might delay or refuse commands that it doesn't like, so, the player must foremost pass the judgement of instinct of the creature.

**Metagame as part of the lore...**

There are many aspects of metagame in the lore. The server itself is technically one: the server is an entity both in real life and within the game universe. There's the player being a player within the lore, and the fact that ShelderEvo is a game both in our reality and within the game's own reality. So, really, you, as a player, can think of the RPG not as playing the role of the creature you adopt, but of the in-game player that adopts it. You can treat this middle-character as simply a connection bridge that you send your commands through, and just focus on the creature, or you might consider the affinity between the player and the creature as your primary gameplay, or you might even focus on the player character and treat creatures as pets, and play a game of 'tame the creature' similar to 'Pokémon' or 'Decktamer'.

### Meta Balancing

'Balancing' is often a central point of discussion in games, especially MMOs. We need to balance unit stats, rarity, rewards, abilities... We strive to avoid 'power-creep' and try to police 'exploits', and it becomes a perpetual battle against the same playerbase that we want to cherish.

Shelder Evolution also struggles to 'balance' the game, and it tries in its own way:

**Evolution as a balancing mechanic?**

Why not let nature run its course? Afterall, didn't nature already figure out how to balance the world? ShelderEvo has 'evolution' in its name, so, clearly, it is involved with evolution and its own mechanics.

The main mechanic of evolution is 'natural selection'. The infamous phrase 'survival of the fittest' might come to mind here, but it is often considered an inaccurate representation of what natural selection does. Is it true that the fittest is the most fit to survive? Duh! But, what does it mean to be 'the fittest'?

When you look closely at the mechanics involved in natural selection, it might become apparent that it is not necessarily rewarding 'the strongest', or 'the most cunning', or 'the smartest', or any other superlative. For any meaningful 'natural selection' to occur, you need a population, an ecosystem for that population to live in, with its limited resources, boundaries, and events, and enough time for events to happen. What it really comes down to is whoever carries on to the next stage, via reproduction, wins, because *they* are the survivors. So, basically, 'the most fertile'. It should really be 'survival of the most fertile'. Evolution can therefore be summarized in 'the struggle to carry on to the next stage via your offspring, by optimizing your build depending on the current meta', which sounds very 'gamey', doesn't it? And that's why this game exists.

By simply existing in the game world, creatures are providing the mixture of resources and events for the ecosystems to self-balance.

**A few key rules to keep things in check...**

Nothing is free in this game. Nothing spawns out of nowhere, or respawns when it expires. Everything has a cost, or is a conversion from one from to another.

When a herbivore forages on plants, they are essentially preying on them, so, that plant's population dwindles. Plants also feed on other organisms and on raw resources, which can expire too. With time, some ecosystem will thrive, thanks to a positive feedback loop or increasing populations, while others will decay, because of that same positive feedback loop running in reverse, causing more and more death to the lack of resources. You can predict the health of an ecosystem by 'counting' the available resources.

Plants with access to sunlight and nutritious soil will flourish, and herbivores with easy access to plants as food will multiply, and predators will have an easy time finding prey becomes the preys replenish their numbers with little effort. In fact, predators become essential in keeping balance of the ecosystem, by trimming the numbers, ergo by keeping that positive feedback loop in check. We could call predators the 'negative feedback loop' that balances the positive one. Or, we could simply call it 'the food chain'.

**The ecosystem tries to self-balance:**

When a player browses the 'adoption screen' (see [adoption](./docs/spec/../../creature_adoption.md)), they are seeing one crucial product of this balancing act.

Regardless of whether that embryo up for adoption is the offspring of creatures raised by other players, or creatures that grew in the wild (a.k.a. 'NPC'), the same mechanics have been applied, so, they occupy a 'slot' in this ecosystem, and their presence is therefore not 'random', they aren't 'spawned' in the classic sense of the word, as in arbitrarily generated from thin air. Their 'spawning' is a result of previous interactions, based on resources being passed around, someone dying, someone else mating... There is an emergent story behind every embryo.

The story doesn't end at adoption, either. Every creature is perpetually converting resources around: creatures eat, digest, and produce waste, which is food for other creatures (e.g. plants), so the cycle can continue. When resources dwindle, creatures will starve, some will die, and, even if it sounds cruel, it will become easier for the survivors.

**'Power creep' as a viable mechanic?**

In RPGs and TCGs, the phenomenon of 'power creep' is when a new entity is designed with some aspect more powerful than the average, either to thrill the playerbase with epicness or to respond to an already happening 'power creep'. But, the moment you create a new piece of the game that can be considered arbitrarily better than the rest, everyone gravitates towards it, and soon, that piece if everywhere. You can see this in every competitive sport, too, especially racing. If a new car model or engine or tyre is overpowered, teams will tend to choose it over the rest, and that model will dominate, turning what was once a varied lineup into a one-model competition. So, the obvious solution is 'balancing', right? We bring the other models up to spec, by introducing new pieces that can compete with that powerful piece, so that the playing field evens out and everyone starts making varied choices instead of sticking to just one piece. But, what we just did, is increase the entire playing field 'power level' up a notch, so that older pieces become irrelevant.

ShelderEvo tries to avoid the 'power creep' phenomenon by not giving in to the urge to balance the playing field — there is no arbitrarily 'better' trait in ShelderEvo. In order for an outlier to emerge, it first needs to 'win the evolution lottery', and, if you read all the above, you might already have a rough idea of how difficult that is by itself. But, say a species manages to get into the right circumstances. First of all, what would those circumstances be like? Well, it would probably have to be a high-population, because we need enough numbers to get a good roll of the dice of genetic mutation during reproduction. Say we get a solid D20. Now what? Well, that just means we get a solid mutation that sticks to the population, which means it can be passed on, and it does get passed on to enough individuals to spread. Now what? Now it needs to be advantageous, but what does that mean? There is no arbitrarily 'better' trait in ShelderEvo, right? So, 'advantageous' just means that it works particularly well in the current circumstances. So, 'winning the lottery' in evolution, really, means winning in more than just one lottery ticket — it's more like 3: the right mutation, to the right population, at the right moment in time.
Change any of these three, maybe it's a mutation that doesn't work in this circumstances, or it's a population that can't really leverage it, or it happens at an irrelevant time, and it breaks. An example would be a metabolism mutation that increases a creature's activity level at the cost of requiring more food more often. Say it happens to a population of small predators, at a time of abundance of prey. This is a lottery — the small predator can now hunt more often, and escape more easily from enemies, thus making its life much easier overall, which means more reproduction, which means they level-up. Now, change one aspect — it's actually a mutation that makes the small predator more sluggish, even though they save energy, but that slugginesh causes them to become easier to catch by bigger predators, and they struggle to catch their own prey. Fail. Or, it happens to a big predator, which can now decimate the population of available prey, until it starves due to the increased dietary needs. Fail. Or, let's change the environment: there is limited prey; the small predator can barely find any more food than before, but now has to eat a lot more to keep the energy up. Fail again. There is no arbitrarily more powerful species to choose from. The most game-breaking 'power creep' you can ever have is a temporary overpowered critter that is currently living in its ideal conditions and is therefore experiencing a boom in efficiency, which also translates in a boom in reproduction. This will often lead to self-balancing, because more critters will eventually enter competition with themselves, a phenomenon we could call 'saturation', meanwhile, the prey population might decrease, thus lowering the saturation level, making the competition even more intense. The critter population will boom, then decay, then it will either stabilize in the current conditions (until the change again) or crash out, thus demonstrating that 'fittest' really means the one that fits the most in the current situation.

**So, it's up to the ecosystem?**

It kinda is. It's the interaction of resources available in the ecosystem, events happening, like weather (storms, droughts, cold snaps, etc.), disasters (e.g. volcanic eruptions, forest fires, floods, etc.), and mutations. These are all 'balanced' mechanics by themselves, in the sense that no single one of these is better than the other, they just provide some variable parameters. It's the interaction of these mechanics that make up the conditions that can turn the tide.

**So, what's the role of a single creature in all of this?**

It all comes down to strategy. How well can that creature leverage the current conditions to complete its quests and 'level-up'? Considering that the main quest of every creature is to live long enough to reproduce, and that 'leveling up' can be translated to passing the traits to the next generation, then, the challenge is not only to survive, but also to reproduce, and to create offspring that are well suited for the new conditions that might arise. So, the 3 lotteries that apply at the ecosystem level, also apply to the individual.
